Structure & Features of a Biography Pages 32–33

What is a Biography? Page 32

  • A true life story.
  • Usually written about someone famous.
  • An information text – it gives facts, but is written in a storytelling style.
  • Always written in the past tense.
  • Written from a third-person point of view (the biographer).
Word Origin: From Greek: bios (life) + graphia (writing).

Example: Florence Nightingale Page 33

Structure:

  • Title: Florence Nightingale
  • Introduction: Famous nurse who changed hospitals.
  • Childhood: Born 1820 in Italy, wealthy family, loved maths and science.
  • Becoming a Nurse: Parents disapproved (low job), but she trained and ran a hospital in London.
  • The Lady of the Lamp: Went to Crimea in 1853, saved soldiers by cleaning wounds and carrying a lamp at night.
  • Later Life: Started a training hospital, wrote books, received medals from Queen Victoria.
  • Her Legacy: Known as the 'mother' of modern nursing.
a. What does the introduction tell you?
Answer: It tells who she is, her profession, and her main achievement (changing how hospitals treated people).
b. How is the information structured?
Answer: Chronologically by subheadings: Introduction, Childhood, Becoming a Nurse, The Lady of the Lamp, Later Life, Her Legacy.
c. What does legacy mean?
Answer: Legacy means what a person is remembered for and the impact they left behind.

P.T. Barnum – The Greatest Show on Earth Pages 34, 35, 38, 39

Reading Part 1 (Pages 34–35) Pages 34–35

Phineas Taylor Barnum was born in 1810. He grew up in the little town of Bethel, Connecticut, in the United States of America (USA).

Every autumn, a man named Hack Bailey and his elephant, Old Bet, came to town. By charging a small fee to see Old Bet, Bailey made a lot of money. From this, young P.T. Barnum learnt that 'When entertaining in public, it is best to have an elephant'.

At 24 years of age, Barnum moved to New York City. He worked with a small travelling circus. After a few years, Barnum wanted to start a long-lasting business, so he bought the American Museum, which he renamed after himself.

Unlike a museum today, Barnum's attraction was part zoo, part theatre, and part 'oddity' show (the audience looked at unusual people and animals).

Barnum heard a story about a woman who claimed to be 161 years old. He hired her for his show. 'Unquestionably the most astonishing and interesting curiosity in the world!' read Barnum's advertisements. Within one week of opening this act, Barnum had made 1500 dollars—a lot of money in those days!

After Mrs Heth died, Barnum found other acts and curiosities—some real, some fake. From jugglers and plate spinners to two-headed cows, P.T. Barnum had all sorts of unusual shows, which he shared with the paying public.

Barnum searched the world for strange things that people would pay good money to see. Then, the great showman came up with the idea that made him world-famous. Barnum decided to put together a circus that would be the best and largest the world had ever seen. The show opened on April 10, 1871, in Brooklyn, New York. It was under a tent that covered three acres. (That's a BIG tent!)

Reading Part 2 (Pages 38–39) Pages 38–39

During the summer, Barnum took the circus on the road. It took 500 men and horses to transport the people, animals and equipment. By 1872, the circus was so big that it had to travel by railroad in 70 freight and passenger cars, pulled by three engines. Barnum called it 'The Greatest Show on Earth'—and it was.

In 1881, James Anthony Bailey joined with P.T. Barnum to create the Barnum & Bailey Circus. Later, they also joined with another circus run by the Ringling brothers.

One of Barnum's biggest successes came when he was in his early 70s. Huge crowds came to see Jumbo, 'The Towering Monarch of His Mighty Race'. Although he didn't name this giant elephant, Barnum's success gave the English language a new word—today, thanks to P.T. Barnum, the word jumbo means 'anything really big'.

Some years later, after he had been ill, Barnum asked a favour of the New York Evening Sun. He wanted the newspaper to print his obituary so he could enjoy it while still alive. The editors of the paper agreed. Less than a month later, on April 7, 1891, P.T. Barnum died at the age of 80.

P.T. Barnum's contribution to entertainment goes beyond just the circus—he is credited with introducing large-scale concerts and public museums to diverse audiences. His museum attracted over 82 million visitors between 1842 and 1868. Today, Barnum's legacy remains a source of entertainment for millions of people worldwide. He reached for the stars, and he is remembered as one of the stars of history.

Sources of Information for a Biography Pages 43–44

Types of Sources Page 44

  • Maps: Showing where the person lived, worked or travelled.
  • Photographs: Of the person and family, friends or colleagues.
  • Family trees: Showing the person's family and ancestors.
  • Letters: From the person to family or friends or colleagues.
  • Online research: Information from books, newspapers and magazines.
  • Interviews: With people who knew the person or with the person himself or herself.
  • Timelines: Showing the chronology of the person's life. They show the order of events.

Women Who Mattered (Part 1) Pages 45–46, 51

A Woman Without Weakness Page 45

Born in 1739, Bibi Rajindar Kaur was a Sikh princess of Patiala, a district in northern India. Her father died when she was only four, and her grandfather and ruler of Patiala, Baba Ala Singh, raised her.

She was married, but her husband died young and left her to manage the family estate of more than 200 villages. She soon became involved in larger issues.

In 1778, her cousin, Raja Amar Singh, was defeated in a battle over territory. Rajindar Kaur gathered a force of 3000 soldiers, and they marched to the aid of her cousin. She offered the same support for her young nephew, Raja Sahib Singh, marching into the city of Maratha at the head of an army. She helped to arrange peace talks with the Maratha leaders.

After a short illness, Rajindar Kaur died in 1791. Her courage, perseverance and wisdom made her the equal of male rulers of her time.
1. What challenging things did Bibi Rajindar Kaur have to do?
Answer: Manage the family estate of 200+ villages, lead 3000 soldiers into battle to help her cousin, and arrange peace talks.
2. What qualities did she have?
Answer: Courage, perseverance, and wisdom.
Vocab: What is the meaning of issues?
Answer: Issues means important topics or problems (e.g., territorial battles and peace talks).

A Woman Astronomer Page 46

Wang Zhenyi (1768–1797) was born and grew up in what is now the province of Nanjing, China, at a time when girls were not allowed to be educated in schools. Her father and grandfather, however, were scholars and encouraged her to read the books in their libraries and educate herself. She devoured works on astronomy, mathematics, geography, medicine, as well as poetry.

In the late 18th century, stars and space were often viewed as supernatural matters. Many people at the time looked to the sky for signs of what the future held. They believed events such as eclipses were gods displaying their anger or displeasure.

However, Wang believed in facts and observation. She set out to learn astronomy and share what she found in clear, simple ways with other scientists and the public.

Wang did not have a laboratory, so she conducted experiments at home. In one experiment, she used a round table as the Earth, a lamp as the Sun and a mirror as the Moon to understand how they moved. She showed what happened in a lunar eclipse and a solar eclipse. Wang showed that the Moon can block our view of the Sun, creating a solar eclipse. She showed that the Earth can block sunlight from reaching the Moon, creating a lunar eclipse.

Wang felt that not allowing women and girls to be educated was unjust and bad for scientific discoveries.
1. How did Wang Zhenyi become educated?
Answer: Her father and grandfather were scholars and let her read books in their libraries to educate herself.
2. What did people around her believe about stars and space? What did she believe?
Answer: People believed stars and space were supernatural and showed signs of the future. Wang believed in facts and observation.
Language Alert: What is the effect of "she devoured works"?
Answer: It is a metaphor that means she read the books very quickly and eagerly, as if she were eating them.
Vocab: What is astronomy the study of?
Answer: Astronomy is the study of the planets, moon, and stars.

Wangari Maathai – A Woman of Vision Pages 48–50

Reading Part 2 (Full Text) Pages 48–50

Wangari Maathai never complained about going to school. She was born in 1940 at a time when few Kenyan girls were fortunate enough to get an education. Her parents were poor farmers in a small village in rural Kenya. After they realised how intelligent Wangari was, they decided to send her to school. Wangari excelled at school and was given a scholarship to study in the United States. Her family didn't have to pay for anything.

After she studied in the United States, she returned and studied at the University of Nairobi. She got a doctorate (PhD), which is the highest qualification you can get at a university. She was the first woman in all of eastern and central Africa to earn a doctorate.

After she finished studying, she got a job at the university to teach veterinary anatomy, which is the study of animal bodies. At the same time, she became interested in the environment.

Wangari noticed that her country was no longer lush, green and fertile. Vegetation was disappearing and there were more droughts. Streams were drying up, forests were being cleared to make way for farms and plantations, and the Sahara Desert was expanding.

She felt that if you saw something and knew it was not right, you need to act to change it. So she decided to do something about the environment.

In 1976, Wangari came up with an idea that could help women while at the same time helping the environment. The idea was simple: she would start a programme that would pay women to plant trees.

Her idea developed into the Green Belt Movement (GBM) which helps poor people and keeps the environment healthy by planting trees.

Wangari understood that trees could do so many things: replenish the soil, provide wood for fuel, protect the water supply, provide nutrition from their fruits and help keep the air clean.

The Green Belt Movement helps rural women who live in poor, rural villages. Women form small groups, name their groups and open group bank accounts. They learn how to plant and care for trees and tree nurseries where trees are grown. Trees are planted in old cans and broken cups and transplanted to tree nurseries after they grow to 30 centimetres.

More than 51 million trees have been planted since Wangari Maathai founded the Green Belt Movement and the countryside is beginning to recover.

Wangari Maathai died on 25 September 2011. She was the first African woman to win a Nobel Peace Prize for her work to improve the environment.

Using a Thesaurus Page 52

What is a Thesaurus? Page 52

  • A special kind of dictionary.
  • Arranged alphabetically (A–Z).
  • Does not list definitions.
  • Lists synonyms (similar meaning) and antonyms (opposite meaning).
Tip: Be careful! Not all synonyms are interchangeable. Angry and furious are synonyms, but furious is a stronger feeling.

Example from text: "...founded the remarkable Green Belt Movement..."

remarkable (adjective)
Syn: amazing, extraordinary, significant, incredible
Ant: average, ordinary, common, mediocre
recover (verb)
Syn: get back, regain, restore
Ant: lose, give up, throw away
